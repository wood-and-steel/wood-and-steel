Wood and Steel · design sketches

Player app redesign

The player app takes over everything on the tabletop player board: which engine is on each token, what each engine carries, and which upgrades you own. It also tracks cash and debt, which players now keep on paper. Game rules and existing app moves stay as they are. These are low-fidelity sketches for discussion, shown at phone size with sample data.

Ground rules

What shaped these sketches

  • Nothing gets recorded twiceIf the table already tracks it, the app doesn't ask for it. Players never report track they build or where engines stand. Recording cargo is optional, and a contract can be fulfilled without it.
  • The app knows active cities, not networksThe board app listens to the player app, never the other way around. So the player app never shows player routes or engine positions. It learns one engine's city only when that engine delivers.
  • Cash never goes negativeA purchase you can't afford says so and stops. The Loans button in the header, on every screen, is the way out. No screen combines borrowing with buying, because players often borrow more than one purchase needs.
  • What's on your board is publicEvery player's cash and debt shows on the home screen. Tapping a player shows the rest of their board: engines, recorded cargo and upgrades.
  • The app doesn't count actionsPlayers keep track of their two actions per turn themselves.
  • Only what changes the outcomeReceipts and summaries list a bonus or a charge only when it changes the number.
Screen map

What connects to what

Every screen and sheet in these sketches, and the tap that opens each one. Three tabs (Home, Commodities, Railroads) and the controls across the top are on every screen. Everything else opens from Home.

  • Commodities and Railroads stay as tabs: they're the reference players check while planning, and Railroads is also where acquisitions happen.
  • The menu holds what's rare: going to the lobby, copying the game code, the round report setting, and the existing playtest tools.
  • Upgrades opens from both the Upgrades row on Home and the Actions sheet. Choose a contract opens after a private delivery and from the Actions sheet's discard.
  • Claiming a market contract can also open the cargo picker, to record what was just picked up.
1 · Home

Your railroad

The screen a player keeps open all game. Everything needed to plan track and run engines fits above the fold on a small phone, market included.

  • The header (cash, debt, ties and Loans) stays on every screen, so borrowing is always one tap away.
  • Under it, each other player's cash and debt.
  • Engines show the token color, engine name and range. Cargo is a plain commodity picker and entirely optional. Set cargo means nothing recorded, not "empty".
  • Contracts are rows, not cards. Each shows its net value to you, with the base value struck through when debt service lowers it. Market rows show the same personal value, plus an expiry badge.
  • Tapping your contract opens the delivery receipt. Tapping a market contract claims it, and the claim offers to record that commodity on an engine. Skipping that is fine.
  • Tapping another player's money opens their details: cash, debt, engines, any recorded cargo, their claimed contract, upgrades, and how far their debt service has advanced. It's the digital version of glancing at their board. Their private contract stays private.
  • The claim sheet lists both engines, says when recording would replace cargo already noted, and offers to skip. If the player already holds a claimed contract, the sheet says only one can be held and offers nothing else. (The claim sketch shows a player who holds none.)
  • Upgrades sit below the fold because players check them less often than contracts.
2 · Delivering

The delivery receipt

Debt service depends on the player, so the receipt shows exactly where the money goes before the player confirms.

  • The app asks which engine delivered only when the answer changes the payout (a Mountain or Challenger against any other engine). That answer is also the one time the app learns where an engine stands.
  • Engine multipliers round down to the nearest $1K before Upgraded Stations is added, as the rules say.
  • If the delivery costs more than the player has, the receipt turns red and says so before they confirm. Bankruptcy is handled by the existing rules.
  • A private contract still leads straight into choosing the next one, as it does today.
3 · Actions

Actions and paying for track

One sheet lists the turn actions that involve the app, each with its price up front. Track is the only cost the app can't know, so the player types the amount in.

  • Borrowing isn't in the actions sheet: it has its own button in the header.
  • Engine buying offers only what the rules allow: one level above your best engine, or two with a Hub for $5K more. The sheet shows the trade-in price next to the full price.
  • The money log lists every change to cash and debt this turn, and the existing turn Undo reverses all of it.
  • The amount field is a plain text input with inputmode="numeric" and pattern="[0-9]*", so phones bring up their own number pad. The ,000 suffix keeps entries in the game's $1K unit.
  • Pay stays tappable even when the amount is too high; tapping it shows the message rather than leaving a greyed-out button with no explanation.
4 · Upgrades

Buying upgrades

The upgrade spaces on the player board become a list. Each row shows its effect in a few words, and its price is the buy button.

  • The Railyard doesn't ask for a city: contract generation ignores it, so its location belongs on the table only.
  • The Hub does ask for a city, choosing from your active cities first, because the app already uses the hub city when it generates contracts.
  • The Commodities Exchange asks which commodity it covers, because a monopoly changes which contracts the app offers. The list holds only the ten commodities that can be monopolized, grouped by how many cities supply them and the ties a monopoly awards.
  • A commodity someone else already monopolized is greyed out with their name, since only the first player can hold it. Each commodity is its own row in the app; the sketch groups some to save space.
  • The app can't see track, so buying the Exchange is the player's word that their track reaches every supplying city. The first player to buy an Exchange for a commodity holds that monopoly.
  • Monopoly ties are awarded at the end of the game, not during play, so they never appear in the header's tie count. Only contract ties count toward the 30 that end the game.
  • Gates the app can check, such as the Service Yard's "any Consolidation on the table", show as locked with the reason. Gates it can't check, such as the Regional Office's five cities, are left to the player.
  • When a row is too expensive it shows how short you are. The way out is the Loans button, not a shortcut on this screen.
5 · Loans

Borrowing and repaying

Reached from the header on every screen. It's the only place that changes debt.

  • Borrowing and repaying are each a turn action. The app doesn't count actions, so players manage that themselves, just as they do on the table.
  • The debt-service progress (East, Central, West) lives here rather than on the home screen, because it matters when deciding how much to borrow.
  • A repayment needs the cash to cover it, so cash never goes negative here either.
6 · End of round

The round report

Shown to every player when a round ends. It replaces the short "Added N routes" message with what to draw and what changed in the market.

  • Each map shows one independent railroad and nothing else: its existing routes thin, this round's routes thick, and only that railroad's cities labeled. No player track or engines, since the player app doesn't have them.
  • The map is cropped to that railroad, with a little margin, so even at phone width it shows only three to six labels. There's no pinch-zoom; the crop already does the zooming. City positions come from the static map geometry, not from board app state.
  • A railroad that grows in two separate places gets one crop that covers both. If that crop gets too wide to read, it splits into two maps under the same name.
  • Nobody is assigned to draw and there are no checkboxes: players handle the map at the table as they do now.
  • The report opens for everyone by default. The switch at its foot turns that off for this player, and the same switch is in the menu so it can be turned back on.
  • With the switch off, the round label at the top gets a dot when a new report is ready. Tapping the round label always opens the latest report, whatever the setting.
  • The setting belongs to the player's device, not to the game, so it never touches shared game state.
  • Later, the same report is a natural home for other end-of-round news, such as monopolies and bonuses.
Sketches use sample data for one player in round 6. Prices and rules come from the current game rules.
